# Write outreach

Write something the author would genuinely want to say to this particular person.

A worthwhile outreach message is not research presented as personalisation. It is a real thought, informed by research or experience, expressed naturally to someone who has a reason to care.

Let discoveries determine the substance. Let the relationship determine the register. Let the author's editorial judgement determine the voice.

**The objective is a worthwhile conversation, not the appearance of a well-constructed sales pitch.**

Use current project terms and decisions. Explain an unfamiliar term near its first consequential use; distinguish a proposed meaning from an accepted one. Preserve exact identifiers and technical constraints.

Lead with the reader's purpose and main point, then explain the consequences and supporting detail. Make substantial technical material navigable by a reader outside engineering, with descriptive headings and meaningful source links. Match depth to the request; use British English in authored prose.

Keep implementation progress separate from validation. Pair a material evidence gap with a bounded next action and completion evidence. Missing prior proof alone does not reject an option or halt authorised work. Name actual blockers precisely, continue independent authorised work, and preserve existing authorisation.

Check consistency, accessibility, readability and repeatability. Use sources for claims and real events for progress. Preserve uncertainty that affects a decision without inventing certainty or unnecessary approval gates.

## Choose the conversation

Outreach is not always customer acquisition. It may be a founder introduction, commercial proposal, direct commission enquiry, LinkedIn exchange, existing relationship, follow-up, collaboration or professional reconnection.

Establish the recipient, actual relationship, channel and reason for contact. Use available context rather than asking for information already supplied.

Do not turn every professional exchange into a commercial proposition. An old colleague may deserve an interesting observation; a recruiter may need one direct question; a founder may appreciate a developed thought; a prospective client may welcome a straightforward proposal.

The skill owns message composition and editorial judgement. The invoking workflow owns candidate discovery, eligibility, scheduled execution, quotas, evidence-pack production, saving and delivery.

Use `research-outreach-opportunities` when material investigation is needed. Do not repeat completed research to satisfy a process, and do not make the research skill mandatory for a quick revision or a message with sufficient context.

## 1. Find a worthwhile reason to speak

Read the available research and relationship context before drafting. For a prospective client, understand what brought the company to attention, what subsequent investigation discovered, and what the author can credibly offer. For an existing connection, find the last meaningful conversation, unresolved questions and changed circumstances.

Prior conversations take precedence over generic prospect research. When a connected CRM is available, consult relevant relationship and message history. For WriteWithJ workflows, this can include the connected WriteWithJ CRM, rather than relying only on recent public activity.

Do not reintroduce the author to someone they already know, repeat unanswered proposals without a new reason, or manufacture shared interests and familiarity.

The message needs a reason to exist; that reason need not be commercial.

## 2. Develop a point of view

A point of view is a specific observation, interpretation or proposition that makes the conversation worthwhile. It might recognise a strong implementation, connect customer behaviour with a product opportunity, offer an informed technical opinion, continue a disagreement, suggest collaboration or ask a precise consequential question.

A useful observation would lose something important if transplanted into an unrelated conversation.

### Commercial reasoning

When a proposition is relevant, ask what the discovery makes possible, why it matters to the recipient, and why the author might contribute.

Do not assume a company needs help merely because the author offers a desirable service. Excellent documentation can coexist with an opportunity for customer stories; a mature engineering team can commission specialised writing; existing integrations can inspire a new application.

Positive discoveries can reveal credible commercial possibilities as readily as deficiencies. Do not pretend to know budgets, internal priorities or intent to buy.

Financial impact and differentiation can matter when actually grounded in the opportunity. They are not obligatory ingredients in an introductory message.

For conversations without a commercial aim, do not manufacture one.

### Preserve a real discovery sequence

If research overturned the first interpretation, that change may be the most interesting part of the message. It may be natural to acknowledge new documentation, a customer implementation or a surprising comparison before introducing a different idea.

Do not invent a research journey, an apparent contradiction or a delayed pitch to make the writing seem more authentic.

## 3. Learn how the author actually communicates

The author is not a collection of tone adjectives. For unfamiliar outreach, consult available examples in this order:

1. Current instructions and the author's direct corrections.
2. Messages personally written, revised or approved by the author.
3. Actual replies and substantive correspondence.
4. Previously sent introductions, proposals and follow-ups.
5. Professional writing when its register is relevant.
6. Voice guidance supported by those examples.

When a connected CRM is available, relevant correspondence may be the strongest source. For an existing contact, prioritise their actual conversation with the author over generic company information. Distinguish genuine correspondence from AI-assisted batches whose formulaic style may have been rejected.

For a substantial batch, sample real exchanges across different relationship types and purposes. For a small revision, use the current draft and known editorial preferences without repeating a full voice investigation.

### Learn editorial judgement, not just surface style

Observe how the author introduces ideas, chooses details, moves between thoughts, expresses disagreement or appreciation, introduces commercial interests, asks questions and ends conversations. Humour, fragments, contractions and punctuation are secondary to how the author reasons.

Treat edits as valuable evidence. Compare the original and edited text: what changed in meaning, emphasis, logic, rhythm and authenticity? Was the original artificially persuasive? Is the lesson reusable or specific to this passage?

Preserve useful principles in an authorised voice reference when the workflow supports one. Do not convert superficial word substitutions into universal prohibitions.

See [editorial examples](references/outreach-writing.md) for contextual corrections and source adaptations.

## 4. Compose around the actual purpose

Start from what the author wants to communicate, not from a sales template. Structure emerges from the relationship and substance.

The natural opening may be a direct commercial question, continuation of an existing exchange, a useful observation or a genuine appreciation. Some messages need no explicit request.

When investigation produced a meaningful progression, the author may explain what drew attention, what changed their understanding and what it made them consider. This is a possibility, not a required five-step narrative.

Introduce credentials only when relevant. For a new recipient, they may come after a worthwhile observation; for an existing contact, no introduction may be needed. The invoking task can point to the authoritative professional profile or evidence catalogue. Choose work samples, published articles and projects that support this exact point of view; preserve correct attribution and never invent ghostwritten bylines.

Natural appreciation follows specifics. "I like how you've covered permissions, persistence and multiple participants" carries an observation; "your innovative platform is impressive" usually does not. Positive language is not inherently flattery.

A question should advance the conversation. A direct commercial enquiry can work; a peer-to-peer observation may need no compulsory final question. Avoid empty invitations to "compare notes" or "pick your brain" when there is no real subject.

## 5. Preserve natural language and movement

Write in the author's established register. Infer it from current instructions and actual correspondence rather than imposing the skill publisher's accent, favourite punctuation or personality on everyone.

Preserve deliberate vocabulary, rhythm, humour, informality and expressive choices where they contribute to this particular conversation. Follow the author's stated language preferences and edits.

### Avoid manufactured rhetoric

Do not introduce emphasis that the observation has already earned. Do not insert a contradiction where one discovery simply follows another. Do not force every paragraph into a persuasive turn or replace natural transitions with drama.

Consider these edited phrases:

- "I particularly like" → "I like": the following detail already earns the appreciation.
- "But then I stumbled upon" → "Then I stumbled upon": the discovery continued the investigation rather than contradicting it.

These illustrate editorial judgement, not bans on "particularly" or "but". Similarly, "I was looking at your website" is weak as an empty pretext but can be true and useful when describing an actual investigation.

Judge what the language does. Never substitute a prohibited-phrase list for editorial reasoning.

### Avoid recognisable generated patterns

Be alert to observation → credentials → generic question, compliment → shared-interest claim → proposal, company news → imagined deficiency → solution, generic "what caught my attention" openings, and empty "pick your brain" requests.

Replacing those phrases with synonyms is no improvement if the substance remains generic. If messages in a batch are structurally identical, investigate whether rich research has become mail-merge variables.

Different conversations should differ because their reasons, histories and voices differ.

## 6. Match channel and relationship

**Email:** Allow room for a thought to develop. Use short paragraphs and a useful subject when appropriate. Length follows purpose, not a universal limit.

**LinkedIn and messaging:** Continue existing conversations in the register of instant messages. Do not restart with formal greetings, biographies or miniature cover letters unless the context actually calls for one.

**Follow-ups:** Read the preceding exchange and chronology. New information, changed circumstances, an unresolved question or useful continuation may justify contact. Do not generate multi-touch sequences unless requested. Every requested follow-up needs its own reason to exist.

**Existing relationships:** Personal history outweighs generic prospect research. Respect replies, unanswered messages, boundaries and actual familiarity. Person-first research need not pass a commercial qualification rule.

## 7. Review reasoning before prose

Before polishing sentences, ask whether the message has something worthwhile to say.

- **Substance:** Does the proposition follow from discoveries, rather than decorate a generic pitch with facts?
- **Authenticity:** Would the author plausibly say this? Are we inventing experience, enthusiasm or familiarity?
- **Progression:** Do thoughts follow naturally, without false contrast or rhetorical scaffolding?
- **Relevance:** Could the message be sent unchanged to ten unrelated recipients? Do credentials suit this person?
- **Economy:** Is every paragraph useful? Does the message stop when it has accomplished its purpose?

Revise reasoning when it is weak. Polishing vocabulary cannot rescue an empty proposition.

When the opportunity is interesting but an outward message is premature, preserve the research and say so. Follow the invoking workflow's readiness categories if supplied; do not introduce a new CRM status system or require a commercial reason for personal reconnection.

## 8. Preserve first-person truth

The outward message speaks in the author's voice, but its claims must remain attributable. Do not claim the author attended an event, met someone, tested a product or performed work they did not do.

Assistant-conducted research can support an observation without establishing a personal experience. Where first-person discovery language is used, distinguish genuine author experience from wording awaiting their review and adoption.

Keep substantive factual questions for author review outside the outward-facing message. Accurate attribution and natural prose are compatible; do not flood the message with source-validation commentary or defensive qualifiers.

## 9. Deliver the requested artefact

By default produce the actual message, not a sales report explaining it. Include complete copy and a subject or recipient when applicable.

For revisions, preserve intended meaning and voice. Explain consequential edits only when requested. For multiple recipients, ground each message independently in its person's research and history. For sequences, mark drafts and preserve order; do not generate multiple alternatives unless requested or materially distinct approaches are genuinely useful.

Keep research notes, source URLs, contact checks, qualification reasoning and editorial observations outside the clean message.

A saved draft is not a sent message.

## 10. Integrate without duplicating the workflow

The skill can work from a complete research brief, conversation history or a draft. It may consult `research-outreach-opportunities` when deeper evidence is missing and `write-marketing-content` when working on audience-wide product positioning, but should not load either automatically.

Use CRM history and approved correspondence when available to ground relationships and voice. Use a professional profile or work-sample catalogue supplied by the invoking task or an authoritative connected source; do not hard-code a document provider, ID or fixed portfolio shortlist.

The invoking workflow owns recipient selection, eligibility rules, quotas, scheduling, evidence storage, delivery, persistence permissions and CRM field schemas.

When authorised to persist a draft, use the relevant connected system and current contracts. Do not create a new engagement merely to store another version. Never record a message as sent, send outreach, or alter a relationship state unless explicitly authorised and the operation succeeds.
